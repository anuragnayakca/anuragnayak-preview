**Updated: August 2026**

For a person living with a disability, planning financially for the future can involve questions that many families do not normally have to think about.

Will there be enough money later in life? Will government disability benefits be affected by savings? How can parents or other family members help? And is there a way to make those savings work harder over the long term?

This is where the **Registered Disability Savings Plan, or RDSP**, deserves attention.

An RDSP is a long-term registered savings plan designed specifically for Canadians who qualify for the **Disability Tax Credit (DTC)**. What makes it particularly valuable is not simply the tax-sheltered growth. Depending on income and eligibility, the federal government can contribute substantial amounts through the **Canada Disability Savings Grant** and **Canada Disability Savings Bond**.

In the right situation, those government contributions can make a significant difference.

## What exactly is an RDSP?

An RDSP is somewhat similar in concept to other registered accounts in that investments can grow within a tax-sheltered environment.

However, an RDSP has a very specific purpose: helping provide long-term financial security for a person with a disability.

There is a **$200,000 lifetime limit on private contributions**, but there is no annual contribution limit. Contributions can generally continue until December 31 of the year the beneficiary turns 59.

The contributions themselves are **not tax-deductible**, unlike an RRSP contribution.

When money is eventually withdrawn:

* the original personal contributions are generally not taxable
* government grants are taxable to the beneficiary
* government bonds are taxable to the beneficiary
* investment growth is taxable to the beneficiary
* certain amounts rolled into the RDSP may also be taxable

Tax is therefore generally deferred while the money remains invested in the RDSP.

## Who can open an RDSP?

The beneficiary must meet four main conditions:

1. Be approved for the **Disability Tax Credit**
2. Have a valid Social Insurance Number
3. Be a resident of Canada when the plan is opened
4. Open the RDSP by December 31 of the year they turn 59

A beneficiary can have only **one RDSP at a time**.

For a child, a parent or legal representative can generally act as the plan holder.

For an adult who is able to enter into a contract, the beneficiary will normally be the plan holder. Different rules can apply where an adult beneficiary cannot enter into the contract independently.

## The first step is actually the Disability Tax Credit

One common misunderstanding is that receiving disability benefits automatically means someone qualifies for an RDSP.

It does not.

The person must first be approved by the Canada Revenue Agency for the **Disability Tax Credit**.

DTC eligibility is based on the effects of an impairment, rather than simply having a particular medical diagnosis or being unable to work.

A medical practitioner must certify the effects of the impairment. CRA considers circumstances such as a severe and prolonged restriction in an eligible activity, the cumulative effect of significant limitations in multiple activities, or certain life-sustaining therapies.

The application can be completed digitally or using **Form T2201, Disability Tax Credit Certificate**.

This is important because DTC approval may sometimes apply to previous years as well. That can create an opportunity to recover unused RDSP grant and bond entitlement from earlier eligible years.

## The part that makes the RDSP particularly powerful: government grants

The **Canada Disability Savings Grant**, or CDSG, matches eligible contributions.

That means an eligible person contributing **$1,500 could have $5,000 added to the RDSP that year**, before any investment growth.

For family income above **$117,045**, the matching rate for 2026 is $1 for every $1 contributed on the first $1,000, providing a maximum annual grant of $1,000.

The lifetime maximum Canada Disability Savings Grant is **$70,000**.

Grant eligibility ends after December 31 of the year the beneficiary turns **49**.

## And some people can receive government money without contributing anything

The **Canada Disability Savings Bond**, or CDSB, is designed primarily for lower and modest-income beneficiaries.

No personal contribution is required.

For 2026:

The lifetime maximum bond is **$20,000**.

This is an important point.

Someone who cannot currently afford to contribute to an RDSP should not automatically assume that there is no reason to open one. If they qualify for the bond, simply opening the RDSP and applying for the government benefit could result in money being deposited into the plan.

## How powerful could the numbers become?

Consider someone who qualifies for the maximum grant and contributes $1,500 each year for 20 eligible years.

Personal contributions:

**$1,500 × 20 = $30,000**

Maximum government grants:

**$3,500 × 20 = $70,000**

That creates:

**$100,000 before investment growth.**

If the beneficiary also qualified over time for the full lifetime Canada Disability Savings Bond of $20,000, the total could reach:

**$120,000 before investment growth.**

Of course, actual grants and bonds depend on income, age, DTC eligibility and other conditions. Investment values will also rise or fall over time.

But the example illustrates why an RDSP deserves to be examined before simply directing all available long-term savings to a regular investment account.

## What if you did not open an RDSP years ago?

This is one of the most useful features of the program.

Unused grant and bond entitlement can generally be carried forward for up to **10 previous eligible years**, provided the beneficiary met the required conditions during those years.

The government automatically tracks the available entitlement.

With carry-forward, as much as **$10,500 of grant** can potentially be paid into an RDSP in one year.

Up to **$11,000 of bond**, including eligible carry-forward amounts, can potentially be received in a year.

This means someone who was approved for the DTC several years ago but never opened an RDSP should not simply start by contributing $1,500.

There may be a better contribution amount.

RDSP holders generally receive a **Statement of Entitlement**, which identifies available grant entitlement and the contribution required to obtain it. Reviewing that statement before making a large contribution can help avoid putting in more money than necessary to capture available grants.

## One rule families really need to understand before contributing

An RDSP is designed for **long-term savings**, not as an emergency account.

If grants or bonds were deposited into the RDSP during the previous 10 years, making a withdrawal can require some government assistance to be repaid.

Generally, for every **$1 withdrawn**, as much as **$3 of grant and bond** may have to be repaid, up to the applicable Assistance Holdback Amount.

For example, withdrawing $5,000 could potentially result in up to $15,000 of recent grants and bonds being returned to the government, depending on what remains subject to the repayment rules.

This is why I would normally look at an RDSP as part of a broader financial plan rather than treating it as the family's only savings account.

Regular RDSP payments must generally begin by December 31 of the year the beneficiary turns 60.

## What happens to disability benefits?

Opening and contributing to an RDSP does not generally affect federal, provincial or territorial disability income benefits.

According to the Government of Canada, withdrawals are also protected in most provinces and territories, although withdrawal treatment may differ in **Quebec, New Brunswick and Prince Edward Island**.

Anyone receiving provincial disability assistance should still confirm the rules applicable to their province before starting significant withdrawals.

## How I would approach RDSP planning

Rather than asking, **“How much can I put into the RDSP?”**, I think the better question is:

**“How much should I contribute this year to make the best use of the available benefits while still keeping enough accessible savings outside the RDSP?”**

A practical approach would normally include the following.

**First, confirm DTC approval and the approval period.**

If someone may qualify but has never applied for the Disability Tax Credit, that is usually the starting point.

**Second, open the RDSP sooner rather than later.**

This becomes especially important as the beneficiary approaches age 49 because grants and bonds stop after that year.

**Third, check available grant and bond carry-forward.**

Someone with several years of unused entitlement may need a very different contribution strategy from someone opening an RDSP for a young child.

**Fourth, capture available grants before making large unmatched contributions.**

If $1,500 attracts $3,500 of grant, for example, contributing an additional $10,000 that year does not automatically produce additional current-year grant.

That extra contribution may still make sense for long-term planning, but it should be intentional.

**Fifth, keep emergency money outside the RDSP.**

The 10-year repayment rules make an RDSP a poor substitute for an emergency fund.

**Finally, invest according to the beneficiary's time horizon and risk tolerance.**

A five-year-old beneficiary potentially has several decades of investment time. Someone approaching the period when withdrawals will begin may require a very different investment mix.

There is no single portfolio that is right for every RDSP.

## Using Empire Life for an RDSP

Empire Life introduced its RDSP in 2025 and describes it as the first RDSP offered by a Canadian life insurance company.

The Empire Life RDSP holds an **Empire Life Guaranteed Investment Funds 75/75 segregated fund contract**.

Investment options range from fixed-income choices through to portfolios with 100% equity exposure, allowing the allocation to be selected according to the investor profile.

The GIF 75/75 contract provides:

* a **75% maturity guarantee**
* a **75% death benefit guarantee**
* automatic annual death-benefit guarantee resets until age 80, subject to the contract terms
* access to a range of segregated fund investment choices

Withdrawals can proportionately reduce the guarantees, and investment values can rise or fall.

Those insurance guarantees can be valuable in certain situations, but they should still be considered alongside investment objectives, fund costs, risk tolerance, expected holding period and other available RDSP providers.

The purpose is not simply to open an RDSP. It is to structure one appropriately for the beneficiary.

## A final thought

An RDSP is one of those accounts where starting with the rules can make a surprisingly large difference.

For an eligible Canadian, the combination of as much as **$70,000 in lifetime grants, $20,000 in lifetime bonds and tax-sheltered investment growth** can create a meaningful source of long-term financial security.

But there are equally important rules around DTC eligibility, age limits, income calculations, carry-forward and withdrawals.

So before contributing, particularly if the beneficiary has several years of unused entitlement, it is worth finding out three numbers:

**How much grant is available?
How much bond is available?
How much contribution is actually required to receive it?**

Those answers can change the entire RDSP strategy.
