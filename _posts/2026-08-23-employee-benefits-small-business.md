An employee benefits plan can include several types of coverage under one workplace arrangement. For a small business, the challenge is deciding what is useful for the workforce while keeping the plan understandable and financially manageable.

A good review starts with the business and its employees, not with the longest list of available benefits.

## What can an employee benefits plan include?

Depending on the insurer and plan design, a group arrangement may include areas such as:

- extended health benefits
- dental benefits
- group life insurance
- short- or long-term disability benefits
- accidental death and dismemberment coverage
- critical illness options where available
- employee assistance or wellness features where available

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada notes that workplace benefits can include health and dental plans, disability or life insurance, and retirement or pension arrangements. The actual coverage depends on the employer's plan.

## Start with the workforce

The same plan design will not fit every company.

Useful questions include:

- How many employees are eligible?
- Are employees full-time, part-time, seasonal or contract?
- Is there a mix of single employees and families?
- What benefits do employees currently use or ask about?
- Is there an existing plan that has become difficult to understand or manage?

Eligibility rules are particularly important. A plan should clearly define who participates and when coverage begins.

## Decide how cost will be shared

The employer and employees may share premiums in different ways depending on the benefit and plan design.

The tax treatment of employer-paid premiums can vary by type of coverage. The Canada Revenue Agency provides guidance on premiums and contributions to insurance plans, including group life, disability and other benefits.

Because plan design and tax treatment can be technical, confirm the treatment of the actual plan rather than relying on a generic rule.

## Look beyond the premium

Premium cost is important, but it is only one part of the plan.

Review items may include:

- deductibles and co-insurance
- annual or lifetime maximums
- drug and paramedical coverage
- dental schedules
- disability waiting periods and benefit maximums
- optional coverage
- dependent eligibility
- termination and continuation provisions

A lower premium can come with different limits or cost-sharing. The goal is to understand those trade-offs.

## Review the plan as the business changes

A plan designed for five employees may not be the right design when the company has twenty-five. Workforce demographics, hiring patterns, budget and benefit usage can change.

That makes periodic review useful even when the company is satisfied with the current insurer.

A review does not automatically require changing carriers. It may result in keeping the plan, adjusting selected features, changing employee communication or simply understanding the current arrangement better.

## Communication matters

Employees can only value a benefit they understand. A plan booklet, onboarding explanation and clear instructions for claims or online access can be just as important as adding another feature.

For employers, the practical objective is a plan that can be explained clearly and administered consistently.
