A new role is usually evaluated through salary, responsibilities, flexibility, commute and career potential. Workplace benefits deserve a place on that list too.

The goal is not to assume that changing jobs creates an insurance problem. It is to understand which benefits end, when new benefits begin, and whether anything you already own continues independently of your employer.

## Start with the old plan

Before your last day, obtain the current benefit booklet or plan summary. Make a simple list of the benefits that matter to you and your family. Depending on the employer, that may include:

- life insurance
- short- and long-term disability coverage
- health and dental benefits
- accidental death and dismemberment coverage
- optional employee-paid insurance
- retirement or group savings arrangements

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada notes that employee benefits can include health and dental coverage, disability or life insurance, and retirement or pension plans. The details are plan-specific, so the employer's plan documents remain the authoritative source for your coverage.

## Compare effective dates, not just benefit names

Two plans can both say "life insurance" or "long-term disability" and still work differently.

When reviewing a new plan, look at questions such as:

- When does coverage begin?
- Is there a waiting or eligibility period?
- Is the benefit based on salary, a fixed amount, or a maximum?
- Who pays the premium?
- Are there optional benefits you need to elect?
- Does the definition of disability differ from your previous plan?
- What happens to coverage if your employment ends again?

A short gap between plans may or may not be important depending on your personal coverage, savings and responsibilities. The useful step is to identify it rather than assume the two plans connect automatically.

## Give disability coverage extra attention

Disability insurance is intended to replace part of your income if illness or injury prevents you from working, subject to the policy definition and other terms. Workplace plans can differ in waiting periods, maximum benefits, offsets, definitions and tax treatment.

If your income is your largest financial resource, it is worth understanding the new plan rather than only checking whether "LTD" appears in the booklet.

## Personal insurance may continue independently

Personally owned insurance is generally separate from an employer's group plan. If you already own life, critical illness or disability coverage, a job change is a useful time to review how that coverage interacts with the new workplace plan.

That does not automatically mean adding more insurance. It may simply confirm that the combination still reflects your income, family, debt and other responsibilities.

## Ask about conversion or continuation options

Some group coverage may offer conversion or continuation choices when employment or group membership ends. Availability, deadlines and terms depend on the plan and insurer.

If those options matter to you, review them promptly rather than assuming they remain available indefinitely.

## A practical job-change checklist

Put the old and new benefit summaries side by side and answer five questions:

1. What ends with my previous employer?
2. When does each part of the new plan begin?
3. What is materially different in amount, definition or maximum?
4. What personal coverage continues regardless of employment?
5. Is there anything I do not understand well enough to make a decision?

The outcome may be that no change is needed. That is still a useful result.
